# Source Translation

Read only the relevant subject family. Preserve the source's actual evidence; these are decision patterns, not motifs to add.

## Shallow-depth flower or botanical scene

- Evidence: principal bloom cluster, stem distribution, depth-of-field behavior, luminous petals, and dominant color.
- Print: encode recurring blossoms as incomplete petal voids, halftone ovals, broken stem axes, and one enlarged contour; do not redraw the bouquet.
- Ink: compress foliage into two or three chromatic clouds and let one stem-direction gesture rise through them.
- Surreal option: a petal contour becomes a large paper opening or the stem rhythm continues beyond the photographed plants as printed intervals.

## Fruit or botanical branch

- Preserve fruit count or rhythm, branch arc, leaf direction, and dominant fruit hue.
- Print count through repeated voids or reduced-color residues.
- Let the branch arc become a structural rule that dissolves into wet pigment.

## Repeated animals or people

- Preserve exact count, order, spacing, body direction, and the surrounding vertical or ground rhythm.
- Print with partial silhouettes, corresponding voids, or fading intervals rather than complete figures.
- Bottom forms may simplify drastically, but count or cadence must remain recoverable.

## Moving street subject

- Preserve sign mass, crossing lines, architectural compression, shadow direction, and dominant motion.
- Use displaced print fragments and broken perspective axes.
- Release the moving figure or vehicle into one sweeping wet action, without inventing a new subject.

## Sparse urban objects

- Preserve object count, intervals, vertical rhythm, railings, long shadows, and perspective.
- Use dry halftone and transfer loss for factual echoes.
- Turn one perspective line or railing rhythm into an impossible continuation through the paper field.

## City panorama or landmark

- Preserve the defining landmark, skyline cadence, roof planes, approach axis, and distant bars.
- Reduce the skyline to incomplete blocks and axes rather than a miniature city drawing.
- Use diluted architectural washes as mass, not literal buildings.

## Traditional architecture and foliage

- Preserve roof silhouette, wall plane, branch direction, and built-form / canopy relationship.
- Do not introduce historical motifs, mountains, mist, seals, or calligraphy absent from the source.
- Allow a roof or branch axis to cross materials; keep the afterimage broad.

## Coast, shoreline, or large water geometry

- Preserve shore contour, step rhythm, horizon, water / land positive-negative shapes, and reflection logic.
- Print contour fragments and interval traces.
- Let a shoreline fold upward or water geometry rise vertically as the controlled impossible event.
- Favor transparent blue-grey, source sand/rock hues, and large paper silence.

## Reflection or pond surface

- Preserve mirror geometry, inverted vegetation masses, surface boundary, and small floating color events.
- Detach or displace the reflection in the printed memory.
- Use pooled washes and tide lines below; do not draw a literal miniature landscape.

## Dense foliage or flower field

- Preserve the principal distribution, repeated rhythm, and any fence, path, or stem axis.
- Aggressively compress micro-detail into a few large print fields and ink clouds.
- Keep broad paper openings so density does not spread across the full sheet.
